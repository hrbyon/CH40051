The misconception list

Wrong answers in this course are not random. Each wrong option in a concept check was written to correspond to one specific belief, and this page names those beliefs. It is not a record of who got what wrong; it is the vocabulary the examinations are written in, and it is published for exactly that reason.

How to use this

When a concept check tells you an answer was wrong, it now also tells you which entry on this page that answer corresponds to. Follow that link. The correct answer is worth less to you than the name of what you actually did.
Stuck on a problem set question? Read the Tell lines. They are written as symptoms: out by a factor of 60, out by exactly n, a voltage that changed when nothing physical did.
Using an AI assistant? These are the checks to run against whatever it hands you, and this is the vocabulary to use when you write down what you changed and why.
Revising means being able to do three things with each entry: recognise it in someone else's work, predict the number it produces, and say what a correct treatment looks like instead. Those are the three question types on the midterm.

Seen in this class

Nothing here yet. An entry moves into this section once at least 6 of the 27 of you have chosen the option that corresponds to it. That threshold is set in advance and applies to everyone: with four options and 27 people, pure guessing puts two or three on each wrong answer, so 6 is the point at which a pattern stops being noise.

Covered so far

Everything the material has reached up to Lecture 1. An entry here is one the questions were designed around — whether this class actually falls for it is what the concept checks are measuring.

M1

Scaling an intensive quantity

Lecture 1
Sounds like"I doubled the half-reaction to balance the electrons, so E° doubles too."
What it isE° is energy per unit charge (J C⁻¹). It does not care how you chose to write the equation. ΔG° = −nFE° is extensive and does scale, because n scales while E° stays put.
TellA cell voltage that changes when nothing physical changed.
M2

Inverted electron ratio

Lecture 1
Sounds like"Cr³⁺ needs three electrons, so I get three times as much chromium."
What it isMoles of metal = moles of electrons divided by n, not multiplied. The number 3 is salient, so the ratio gets inverted in the direction that feels generous.
TellYour answer is out by a factor of exactly n, or of n².
M3

"Non-spontaneous" read as "impossible"

Lecture 1
Sounds like"ΔG° is positive, so this reaction cannot happen."
What it isΔG° is defined at standard state. Spontaneity is governed by ΔG, not ΔG°. Change the concentrations, or apply a voltage, and the reaction proceeds. Almost all industrial electrochemistry lives here.
TellThe word "impossible" appearing anywhere in your answer.
M4

Charge confused with current

Lecture 1
Sounds like"The current was the same in both cells, so the same amount was deposited."
What it isWhat is deposited follows the charge, Q = I·t, and then the equivalent weight M/n. A current on its own says nothing until you multiply by a time — in seconds.
TellOut by a factor of 60, 3600, or by whatever the two run times differ by.
M9

Polarity label mistaken for the definition

Lecture 1
Sounds like"The anode is the negative terminal, so in an electrolytic cell the anode must be different."
What it isAnode and cathode are defined by the reaction: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode. The (+)/(−) label answers a separate question about which way the electrons are being pushed, and it flips between cell types while the definition does not.
TellAny sentence where the sign of a terminal is used to decide what reaction happens there, or which way an ion moves.
M10

Direction-blind E°

Lecture 1
Sounds like"E° for copper is +0.34 V, so it is +0.34 V whichever way I write the reaction."
What it isA tabulated E° belongs to a written direction — by convention, reduction. Reverse the reaction and the sign flips. This is a different slip from M1: here the direction is lost, not the scale.
TellA cell potential that comes out negative when you already argued the cell is spontaneous, or two half-reactions added without either being reversed.
M13

Double sign flip at the anode

Lecture 1
Sounds like"I reversed the anode half-reaction to +1.66 V, then used E°cell = cathode − anode."
What it isPick one route and stay in it. Either keep the tabulated reduction potentials and subtract, or reverse the anode reaction and add. Doing both changes the sign twice, so the anode ends up contributing the wrong way.
TellYour E°cell is smaller than either half-reaction suggests, or a cell you argued was spontaneous comes out with a suspiciously small positive voltage.

What the examinations do with this

The midterm is written in this vocabulary. Three kinds of question, and only the first can be answered from memory:

  · here is a belief — what number does it produce for this cell?
  · here is a solution containing no error — justify each step;
  · here is a solution you have not seen — find what is wrong and name it.

The final hands you a real set of measurements with a fault buried in it and asks you to diagnose the fault, prove it from the data, and design the experiment that would settle the question. Every entry on this page is a rehearsal for that.

A warning about assistants. An AI assistant will find the arithmetic in a broken solution almost every time. What it does poorly is name the belief underneath in the terms this course uses — it tends to answer "calculation error" and stop. Marks here are attached to the second thing, so an answer you pasted without checking will read as generic even when it is not wrong.
